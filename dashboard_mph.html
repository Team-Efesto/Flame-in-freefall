1. Lunar Fluid Dynamics (0.165 g_e)

On the lunar surface ($g = 1.62\text{ m/s}^2$), the buoyant velocity of hot gases drops to $U_B \propto g^{0.5} \approx 0.406\,U_{B,\text{Earth}}$ (~$44\text{ cm/s}$ instead of $108\text{ cm/s}$). As a result, the thermal boundary layer thickness and the flame standoff distance $\delta_f \propto p^{-0.5}g^{-0.25}$ increase by +57% at 101 kPa and up to +110% at 56.5 kPa.

2. The Artemis Atmosphere Paradox (56.5 kPa • 34% O₂)

To prevent decompression sickness before extravehicular activities (EVA), Artemis lunar modules operate at reduced pressure ($56.5\text{ kPa}$) but enriched to 34% $\text{O}_2$. While the low pressure reduces the Grashof number ($Gr \propto p^2g$), the high $\text{O}_2$ mole fraction cuts the chemical time (high $Da$), making materials such as Nomex or Kapton significantly more flammable than in Earth air.

3. Confinement & Detection in the MPH Module

In the cylindrical MPH volume ($\sim 48\text{ m}^3$), the weaker buoyancy makes the smoke plume rise more slowly and spread sideways before reaching the ceiling, delaying the activation of standard optical detectors but increasing the risk of radiative interaction with the walls (NASA PSI-25 effect).
